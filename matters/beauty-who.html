Who books: new versus returning clients, and what each needs to hear

By ContentMatter · 2 min read · Published September 24, 2026 · Updated September 30, 2026

A first time client is deciding whether to trust you. A returning client has already decided and wants an easy way back. Treat them as two audiences with two jobs.

New clients are comparing

MyAdvice's survey found that 63% of people researching med spas compare between two and five before choosing, and 55% are less likely to choose one with incorrect information online. New clients want proof: recent reviews, provider credentials, clear service descriptions, and a straightforward way to ask questions. Put those in front of them first.

Returning clients are worth protecting

Harvard Business Review reported Bain & Company research showing that a 5% increase in retention can raise profits by 25% to 95%. That research spans many industries, but the idea applies to appointment businesses, where a loyal client books again and recommends you. Make rebooking easy with a link in your confirmation message and a reminder timed to the treatment cycle.

Tailor the message

For new clients, answer the questions that stop a booking: what is the process, who will treat me, what does it cost, and what happens if I have a concern. For returning clients, make the message short: your next appointment is ready, here is the link.

Use the booking data

Google Business Profile reports bookings when your appointments run through a booking provider. Compare new and returning bookings by source. If most new clients come from search and most returning clients from email, you know where each effort belongs.

Keep records of what works

Note which offers, messages, and services bring each group back. Over time, those notes become a plan.

An illustrative example

A dental practice sends the same email to everyone on its list. New patients feel it is too casual, and existing patients feel it repeats information they already know. After splitting the list, new patients receive an introduction to the team, what to expect at a first visit, and how to book. Existing patients receive a reminder with a direct booking link. Response improves because each person gets the message that fits.

Common mistakes to avoid

Treating every inquiry the same way, making returning clients repeat their details, hiding prices and process from new clients, and forgetting to thank people for coming back. Each group has a different worry, so answer that worry first.

A simple plan for the next 30 days

Week one, tag your client list as new or returning. Week two, rewrite your confirmation and follow up messages so each group hears what it needs. Week three, add a rebooking link and a reminder timed to the typical treatment cycle. Week four, compare return visits and new bookings against last month. Keep a short note on which messages worked for which group, and build your next quarter around those findings rather than around assumptions. Over a few cycles, you will see which group is growing and which one needs more attention.

Try this first

Split last quarter's bookings into new and returning clients. Write one sentence on what each group needs from you, then update your confirmation message accordingly.
