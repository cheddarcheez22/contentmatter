Who your regulars are: locals, office crowds, and families

By ContentMatter · 2 min read · Published September 17, 2026 · Updated September 30, 2026

Your best guests share patterns: where they live or work, when they come, and how they found you. Describe those patterns and you can reach more people like them instead of everyone.

Start with the guests you already have

Look at your top repeat guests from the last quarter. Where do they come from? What time do they visit? Do they come alone, with colleagues, or with family? Even a rough profile of three groups, such as nearby workers, local families, and weekend groups, is enough to start.

Price pressure shapes behavior

The National Restaurant Association's 2026 report found that more than 7 in 10 consumers say they would use restaurants more frequently if they had more disposable income, and that Gen Z and millennials lead off premises growth. Value, convenience, and takeout or delivery options matter to these groups, so your offers should speak to them directly.

Friends bring friends

BentoBox's Diner Discovery Report found that 56% of social media users find restaurants through recommendations from people they know. Give each group a reason to bring someone: a shareable dish, a group menu, a simple referral message at the end of a receipt.

Speak to each group on its terms

Workers nearby want fast, reliable lunch and easy ordering. Families want space, flexible seating, and menu options for different ages. Weekend groups want atmosphere and a reservation they can trust. Write one short message per group and test which one earns the most visits.

Keep the list current

Update your guest list and your notes every month. Remove contacts who never engage, and add notes on what each group responds to.

An illustrative example

A restaurant reviews its repeat guests and finds three patterns: office workers on weekday lunches, families on Sunday afternoons, and small groups on Friday nights. It writes a short lunch message, a family message, and a group message. Each goes to a different list with a different time of day. The lunch message is shorter and mentions speed. The family message mentions seating and kid friendly dishes.

Common mistakes to avoid

Calling everyone "customers," guessing instead of looking at your data, copying a competitor's audience, and sending the same message to a whole list. A simple three group model is often enough to find what works.

A simple plan for the next 30 days

Week one, export your repeat guests or review recent orders and look for patterns by time, group size, and order. Week two, define three guest groups and write what each one wants in one sentence. Week three, send each group a message that fits, with one clear action, and track which one gets the strongest response. Week four, review and keep the best performing message. Repeat each quarter, because the groups change as the neighborhood changes. Share the findings with your front of house team, who often know the regulars better than any report does.

Try this first

Name your three most valuable guest groups. For each, write one sentence about what they want and one way to reach more of them.
